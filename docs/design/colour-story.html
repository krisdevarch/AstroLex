Silence is grey. Every word you restore brings a colour back.

The letters are glass, and the backgrounds are painted skies, like the reference you sent. The glass picks up each act's colours, so every act looks different, and the letter inside stays crisp white so it always reads.

The two references

What we take from Ori and from Hades

Ori and the Blind Forest: heart and light

  • The player cares before they learn. Open with a wordless prologue, and make water the first word on screen.
  • You restore rather than destroy, and the world visibly heals behind you.
  • Light carries meaning: the dark lifts as you catch letters.
  • The villain is grieving. Babel chooses to listen.

Hades: character, colour and memorability

  • Every character has one signature colour, used everywhere they appear.
  • Bold, saturated accents on a dark base read in motion and stand out in a feed.
  • Losing still moves the story on.
  • The game notices what you did. Babel's lines are built from your letters.
Why glass

Letters nobody else has

Most word games use a flat cream tile with a dark letter, as on the left. A glass tile, like Apple's Liquid Glass, bends and blurs the sky behind it. The letters then look like part of space, not stickers on top of it, and they change with every scene.

How a glass tile is built

Five layers

  1. RefractionThe sky behind the tile is blurred, brightened and slightly warped by the tile's shape. This is what makes it glass.
  2. Frost coreA soft dark frost sits behind the letter, so the glyph has a calm patch whatever the sky does.
  3. GlyphA white glyph with a thin ink halo. It always faces the player and never changes colour.
  4. SpecularA highlight from the visor lamp slides across as the tile tilts. This is the Ori light.
  5. Edge tintThe rim and the bottom of the glass pick up the act's accent colour. When the tile is caught, the glass fills with that light.

In Godot this is one canvas_item shader that reads the screen behind the tile (one back-buffer copy per frame for all tiles), uses the tile's normal map to warp it, and samples a lower mip level for the blur. It runs on the Compatibility renderer and on the web. The first test is the frame rate on your iPhone with 20 glass tiles on screen.

Five colour rules

The system

  1. The glyph never changes colour. White with an ink halo on frosted glass, in every act. The glass around it takes on the act's colour.

  2. Each act owns a palette and a scene. A painted sky, one landmark (Earth, a nebula, the Tower, Babel's core) and layered silhouettes, in at most four parallax layers.

  3. Each character owns one colour. It shows on their chat bubble, portrait rim, name and leitmotif.

  4. Colour is the reward. Each caught letter brightens the sky a step, and a finished word relights a district of the Silent City.

  5. 60 · 30 · 10. 60% deep sky, 30% glowing mid-tones (the band of stars, the landmark), 10% light: glass, tether and character accents.

Readability guardrails

  • Glass is pretty but can hurt reading, a criticism Apple's Liquid Glass also got. The frost core and the ink halo keep the glyph at 4.5:1 contrast or better against the worst patch of every sky. Check this with a script, not by eye.
  • Frost amount, blur and edge tint are tunables. The reduced-transparency setting turns the glass frosted and opaque.
  • The sky behind the play area stays calm. The busy parts (the landmark, the band of stars) sit above and below where tiles drift.
  • No signal relies on colour alone.
